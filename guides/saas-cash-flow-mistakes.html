A forecast is a decision system, not a report

Most cash forecasts are written to be read. The useful ones are built to be acted on. The difference shows up in one question: when the number moves, does anyone do anything? A report gets circulated, nodded at, and filed. A decision system connects every line of the cash view to something a specific person can change — a collection call, a delayed start date, a renegotiated payment term, a financing conversation started early instead of late.

This distinction matters because runway is the one number a founder cannot afford to be wrong about, and it is distorted more often by process mistakes than by math mistakes. The math in a cash forecast is addition and subtraction. The process — what feeds the model, how often it is refreshed, who acts on what it says — is where the distortion creeps in. The five mistakes below are the ones we see most. Each one makes runway look healthier than it is, which means each one steals the time you would have used to act. A company that believes it has ten months of cash behaves differently than a company that knows it has seven, and the behavior gap is exactly the set of decisions — hiring pace, collections pressure, financing timing — that would have fixed the problem while it was still fixable.

After the mistakes, we will build the tool that corrects all five: the 13-week cash-flow forecast.

Mistake 1: Confusing bookings with collections

Signed is not collected. A closed deal produces a contract; the bank account only moves when the invoice goes out, the customer's AP process runs, and the payment clears. Between those two events sit payment terms, procurement reviews, missing PO numbers, invoice disputes, and customers who simply pay slow. ARR can be growing briskly while cash gets tighter every week, because the operating story and the bank balance run on different clocks.

This mistake is most dangerous in exactly the companies that feel best about themselves. A strong sales quarter fills the forecast with cash that has not arrived, and the confidence it produces funds hiring against money that is still sitting in someone else's AP queue. Then the collections lag arrives all at once, and the company discovers it spent the quarter's cash before receiving it.

The fix is definitional discipline. Keep three questions separate at all times: what was earned, what was billed, and what was collected. A forecast that feeds on bookings will always look better than reality, and it will look best right before it is wrong. Forecast collections from invoices and payment behavior — never from the sales pipeline.

Mistake 2: Blended monthly averages hide the weeks that hurt

A monthly model shows burn as one smooth number. The bank account experiences something spikier: payroll lands on specific days, and on a biweekly cycle some months carry three payrolls instead of two. Rent hits on the first. An annual software renewal lands in one week, not spread across twelve months. Debt service comes due on its own schedule, indifferent to yours.

Average those into a monthly line and the model can show positive cash for a month in which the company actually goes below its comfort line for ten days in the middle. Companies do not run out of cash on average — they run out on a Thursday. Any forecast that cannot see individual weeks cannot see the Thursday coming.

The weekly view also changes behavior in a way the monthly view never does. When leadership can see that the tight week is the second week of next month, the response is specific: pull a collection forward, push a discretionary payment back, hold a start date two weeks. When all leadership can see is "next month looks tight," the response is a meeting.

Mistake 3: Ignoring AR aging drift

Receivables age quietly. A customer that paid in thirty days starts paying in forty-five, then sixty. No single invoice looks alarming, so nothing triggers a conversation — but the collections curve has shifted underneath the forecast, and every future week now assumes cash arriving earlier than it actually will. Multiply that drift across the customer base and the runway number is stale even though nobody changed a single assumption.

The correction is to treat the AR aging report as a forecasting input, not an accounting artifact. Review it weekly. When an account's payment behavior slips, the forecast slips with it — and someone owns the call to that customer. Drift that is watched is a collections task. Drift that is unwatched is a runway error.

Mistake 4: Treating one-time inflows as run-rate

An annual prepayment, a tax credit, a one-time services engagement, a financing draw — each of these is real cash, and each of them lies about the future if it slides into the run-rate. The month looks strong, the trailing average improves, and the forecast quietly extends runway on the assumption that something unrepeatable will repeat.

Tag every inflow as recurring or one-time at the moment it is forecast, and keep the tags when it lands. The honest question for any strong cash month: how much of this arrives again next month without anyone doing anything new? That answer is the run-rate. Everything else is a windfall, and windfalls fund decisions — they do not change the trend line.

Mistake 5: Forecasting on accrual instead of cash timing

Accrual accounting answers an important question — how is the business performing? — and it is the wrong tool for a different question: when does money move? Revenue recognized over twelve months may have been collected up front, or not yet at all. A prepaid expense hit the bank months before it hits the P&L. A bonus accrual builds all year and leaves the account in one week.

A forecast built from the P&L inherits all of these timing fictions at once. Build the cash forecast from cash events: invoices and their expected payment dates, payroll runs on their actual dates, vendor payments on their actual terms. Keep the accrual view for performance and the cash view for survival, and reconcile the two so you can explain the gap — but never let one impersonate the other.

A forecast earns trust when a founder can point to the next decision — not just the next number.

Centripetal operating principle

The 13-week cash-flow forecast: the tool that corrects all five

The 13-week forecast is the single most useful operating tool an early-stage company can run, and it is deliberately unglamorous: one spreadsheet, thirteen columns, refreshed every week. Thirteen weeks is one quarter — far enough out to see a problem while there is still time to act, near enough that the numbers stay honest. The near weeks are precise; the far weeks are directional; the weekly refresh keeps pulling the far weeks into focus.

The structure

The columns are weeks — the next thirteen of them. The rows follow the shape of the bank account:

  • Beginning cash. Reconciled to the actual bank balance every week, restricted balances noted separately. Not the model's opinion of the balance — the bank's.
  • Collections. Built from the AR aging plus expected invoicing, timed by each customer's real payment behavior. Not bookings. Not pipeline. Cash you can name, from invoices that exist or are scheduled to exist.
  • Disbursements. Payroll on its actual run dates, rent, debt service, vendor payments on their actual terms, and one-time items — annual renewals, tax payments, deposits — placed in the specific week they hit.
  • Net change. Collections minus disbursements, by week.
  • Ending cash. The number that matters, thirteen times, and the input to next week's beginning cash.

The weekly refresh and variance review

The forecast is rebuilt every week, and the first step is looking backward: compare last week's actuals to what last week's forecast said would happen. Every material variance gets the same treatment — what moved, why, an owner, and a decision. A customer paid late: who calls them, and does their timing assumption change for every future invoice? A disbursement landed early: was it a one-off or a term change? Variances are not embarrassments to explain away; they are the forecast teaching you how the business actually behaves.

Run the review at the same time every week, with the person who owns collections and the person who can authorize spending decisions in the room. It should take well under an hour once the rhythm sets in. The output is not the spreadsheet — it is the short list of decisions and owners the spreadsheet surfaced.

What good looks like after a month

The tell that the system is working: variances shrink. Week over week, the forecast's misses get smaller because every miss taught it something — this customer pays in fifty days, not thirty; that vendor debits a week early. And decisions start happening earlier, off the forecast, instead of later, off the bank balance. That is the whole point. The forecast did not get more accurate as an end in itself; it got accurate enough to run the company on.

How the 13-week forecast quietly dies

Most companies that abandon the 13-week forecast do not decide to abandon it. It erodes, usually in one of four ways.

It becomes a finance-only artifact. The forecast gets built, circulated, and never discussed with anyone who can change it. The person who owns collections never sees the collections assumptions; the person approving offers never sees what a new hire does to week nine. A forecast that only finance reads is a report again — the exact thing it was built not to be.

The refresh gets skipped in good months. Cash is comfortable, the review feels unnecessary, and the forecast goes stale precisely when the discipline is cheapest to maintain. Then a tight stretch arrives and the tool that was supposed to see it coming is six weeks old. The weekly refresh is the product; a 13-week forecast refreshed monthly is a monthly forecast with extra columns.

Bookings creep back in. Under pressure to make the picture look better, someone starts sliding expected deals into the collections row. It works — the picture looks better — and the forecast is now lying in the direction that hurts most. Collections come from invoices and payment behavior. If a number in that row cannot be traced to a customer and a date, it does not belong there.

Variances get explained as "timing." Every miss is timing in some sense; the word explains nothing. A variance review where "timing" closes the discussion produces no owners and no decisions, and within a month the review is a formality. Hold the standard: what moved, why, who owns it, what we are doing.

The pattern across all four is the same. The forecast survives as long as it stays connected to decisions and named owners, and it dies the moment it becomes something the company produces instead of something the company uses. Guard that connection and the rest of the mechanics take care of themselves.

Frequently asked questions

Why can SaaS runway look wrong?

Runway can be distorted when the forecast uses inconsistent cash definitions, ignores collection timing, or treats committed costs as if they were optional.

What should a weekly cash review include?

Beginning cash, collections, payroll, vendors, taxes, financing obligations, ending cash, and the decisions that could change the next four to thirteen weeks.

When does a founder need senior finance help?

When cash decisions depend on a model nobody fully trusts, financing choices are approaching, or operating leaders cannot see tradeoffs early enough to act.
