The First 90 Days After Your Raise

A post-close operating playbook for SaaS founders: what to set up before the capital starts deploying.

Use the first 90 days after a raise to connect treasury, reporting, forecasting, and decision ownership before the new capital starts deploying. The goal is a repeatable operating rhythm that keeps the cash view and board view aligned.

The wire hits. Now what?

The first 90 days after a raise decide whether your financial infrastructure scales with the company or becomes the bottleneck everyone works around later. Most founders spend this window on hiring and product. That is understandable — the round was raised to build. But the finance work that does not get done here becomes debt with a compounding rate: a treasury structure improvised under pressure, a board cadence invented the week before the first meeting, a forecast that is still the fundraise deck wearing a different filename.

The fundraise model deserves its own sentence. It was built to persuade. It showed the version of the company that got the round closed. That is its job, and it did it. It is not an operating tool. The longer it doubles as one, the wider the quiet gap grows between what you told investors and what the bank account is doing — and you will eventually have to explain that gap in a board meeting.

The playbook below splits the 90 days into three phases. Each phase ends with something specific existing — not planned, not scheduled, existing. For each phase: what should be in place by the end of it, who owns it, and the failure mode we see most often.

Days 0–30: Treasury structure, cash controls, and a forecast that reflects the round

What should exist by day 30

A treasury structure that separates operating cash from reserves. The round should not sit in a single business checking account. Operating cash — roughly what you will actually spend in the near term on payroll, vendors, and infrastructure — stays liquid in the operating account. Everything above that moves to reserve: an insured sweep program, money market, or short-duration treasury structure appropriate to your liquidity needs. Remember that standard FDIC coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category — for most rounds, that is a small fraction of what just landed. Concentration in one bank is a decision; make it deliberately or not at all. One caution before moving a dollar: if the company has any debt facility or line of credit, read the deposit covenants first. Some agreements require operating deposits to stay at the lender. Sweeping cash out without checking can put a performing company in technical default.

Cash controls configured before anyone is under pressure. Dual approval on wires above a threshold you set. Two-factor authentication on every login with money-movement authority — every signer, no shared credentials. A written rule that new vendor payment instructions get a verbal callback to a known number before anything is approved. These take an afternoon to set up now. They take much longer to set up after the first fraudulent wire request, and by then the conversation is different.

An operating forecast that reflects the round you actually closed. Rebuild the forecast on the real numbers: the amount that actually hit the account net of fees, the hiring plan with real start dates and fully loaded costs, and revenue assumptions you would defend to your board rather than pitch to a new investor. Build it as a scenario forecast tied to management decisions — the question it must answer is "what changes if we hire faster, slower, or differently" without a rebuild. If pulling a hire forward two months requires re-architecting the model, the model is a document, not a tool.

A standing cash report. Someone should see the cash position on a defined schedule — daily or weekly, but defined. Not "when someone checks the bank portal." The operators we respect most treat the cash report the way pilots treat the fuel gauge: glanced at constantly, boring almost always, and never optional.

Who owns it

The founder or CEO signs the treasury policy and stays a signer on the accounts — approvals of material money movement cannot be fully delegated, and should not be. The mechanics — opening accounts, configuring sweeps, setting up controls, rebuilding the model — belong to the finance lead, whether that is a controller, a fractional CFO, or an embedded finance partner. The division is simple: finance builds the machine, the founder holds the keys.

The most common failure mode

The round sits in one checking account for months because nobody owned the decision to move it. Controls get postponed because they feel like bureaucracy for a twelve-person company — until the first convincing fake-vendor email arrives, or the first diligence request asks how wires get approved and the honest answer is "the founder sends them from his phone." And the forecast stays the fundraise model, which means every operating decision for the next two quarters gets measured against a document that was never meant to be true.

Days 31–60: Reporting cadence, board rhythm, and the hiring plan

What should exist by day 60

A monthly close on a fixed timeline. Pick a day of the month the books close and hold it. The close does not need to be fast yet; it needs to be predictable. A close that lands on a different day each month is not a cadence, it is a surprise with a spreadsheet attached.

A KPI set defined once, then left alone. Revenue by customer by month, net burn, cash and runway, pipeline, and whatever two or three metrics actually describe your motion. The specific list matters less than the fact that it is fixed. A board that sees different metrics each quarter cannot see a trend, and a trend is the only thing a board can actually help with.

The board deck template, built before the first board meeting. Not the deck — the template. The sections, the order, the metrics that appear in the same place every time. Set the board calendar for the next twelve months while you are at it. The narrative format your board sees in month two is the format they will expect for the next two years, so choose it deliberately instead of inheriting whatever got produced under deadline.

The hiring plan reconciled against the model. Every open role, with a start date and a fully loaded cost, tied line by line to the forecast. Count offers, not just start dates — an accepted offer is a cash commitment whether or not the person has badged in. This reconciliation is where most post-raise plans quietly diverge from reality, so do it in writing, monthly.

Vendor and spend controls. Approval thresholds by amount. A corporate card program with per-employee limits instead of reimbursement sprawl. A defined process for adding a new vendor. None of this is heavy; all of it is easier to install now than to retrofit at fifty people.

Who owns it

Leadership owns its own board material. The sales leader owns the pipeline slide. The product leader owns the roadmap slide. Even if it is plain text with no graphics, they write it and they present it. When finance is assembling every department's slides, the company does not yet have a management team — it has a reporting department with a distribution list. Finance owns the calendar, the close, and the consistency of the numbers. The CEO owns the narrative — including the bad news, delivered early and plainly. Bad news surfaced by management builds trust; bad news discovered by the board spends it.

The most common failure mode

Reporting exists but cadence does not. The close happens when it happens. The deck gets assembled the week of the meeting, by finance, alone, at night. And hiring outruns the model quietly — offers go out ahead of the plan because each one individually seemed reasonable, and no one reconciled the stack of them against the forecast until the burn number came in high and nobody could say exactly why.

Operating principle

A board meeting should end with owners, dates, and metrics — not with alignment. Alignment is how a meeting feels. Owners and dates are what a meeting produced.

Days 61–90: The first board package and the locked cadence

What should exist by day 90

A first board-ready reporting package that ships without heroics. Cash position and runway. Actuals against plan for revenue, burn, and hiring. The fixed KPI set. And commentary in plain language — what moved, why it moved, and what management is doing about it. If the package requires a week of all-hands scramble to produce, the underlying cadence is not real yet; the package is the test of the first sixty days, not a separate project.

Variance review as a discipline, not an apology. Every material variance from plan gets the same three sentences: what moved, why, and what we are doing about it — with an owner and a date attached to the third sentence. A variance explained as "timing" with no owner is not explained; it is deferred. The tone matters as much as the content. The goal is not to be sorry about the burn. The goal is to own the number with a plan attached.

A locked operating cadence. The weekly cash view, the monthly close and financial review, the rolling forecast update, and the board rhythm — all on the calendar, all with named owners, all designed to run when the founder's attention has fully returned to product and customers. That last clause is the whole point. A cadence that depends on founder attention is a hobby.

Who owns it

Finance owns the package and the review meeting. Each functional leader owns their variances — the sales miss belongs to the sales leader, not to the spreadsheet. The CEO owns the decision that comes out of each material variance, because a variance review that produces no decisions is a book club. By day 90, the founder's job in the financial cadence should have shrunk to two things: showing up prepared, and making the calls the review surfaces.

The most common failure mode

The first board package is a data dump — forty pages, no narrative, every metric the tools could export. Boards do not want more data; they want to know what management sees in it. The second failure is softer: the cadence holds for one cycle, then drifts. The close slips a few days, the variance review becomes an email, the forecast refresh waits for the next fundraise. Nobody decides to stop; it just stops. The fix is structural, not motivational — named owners, recurring calendar entries, and a founder who asks for the cash report when it is late.

The question to ask at day 90

End the first quarter after the raise with an inversion: what information did we not have in the last 90 days that would have helped? Ask it in the financial review, write the answers down, and fix the visibility gaps while they are cheap. In our experience the answers are rarely exotic — revenue by customer by month, the gap between booked and collected, the forward cost of headcount already committed. The stuff you assumed you had until you reached for it.

Then apply the day-91 test: could you walk into a board meeting tomorrow, on a day's notice, with the standing package and nothing custom-built? If yes, the raise bought you an operating company, not just runway. If no, you now know exactly which phase to go back and finish.

Frequently asked questions

What should happen first after a SaaS raise?

Set up treasury structure, cash controls, reporting ownership, and the operating forecast before the capital deployment plan takes shape.

What should be in place by day 90?

A monthly financial review, rolling forecast update, board-ready reporting cadence, and cash-management structure with documented controls and review dates.
