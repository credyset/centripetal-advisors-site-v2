What Actually Happens When You Breach a Covenant

Founders fear covenant breaches like they fear board termination. In practice, the conversation is usually more measured than the anxiety.

A covenant breach does not automatically mean default, acceleration, or the loss of the facility. In our experience, lenders ask three questions: what happened, what is the plan, and when will the company return to compliance. The quality of those answers determines the outcome more than the breach itself.

The fear is worse than the event

Most founders have never breached a covenant, so their mental model comes from contract language: "event of default," "acceleration," "cross-default." Those terms are real, but they describe the lender's rights, not the lender's likely response.

In practice, lenders are in the business of getting repaid. A performing loan with a temporary covenant miss is better for the lender than an accelerated loan that forces a fire sale. Lenders know this. The conversation that follows a breach is usually practical, not punitive.

The breach itself is not the problem. The problem is surfacing it late, without a plan, in a way that makes the lender question whether the company knows its own numbers.Centripetal operating principle

What the lender actually asks

When a borrower reports a covenant breach — or when the lender discovers it in a compliance certificate — the conversation typically covers three things.

First: what caused the miss. A revenue timing issue, a one-time expense, a customer churn event, or a seasonal pattern. The lender is trying to understand whether the breach is structural or temporary.

Second: what the plan is. A remediation plan should show the specific actions the company is taking, the timeline to return to compliance, and the operating assumptions behind both.

Third: what changed in the model. The lender will want to see an updated forecast that reflects the new reality, not the original plan that assumed the covenant would never be tested.

The waiver process

If the breach is temporary and the plan is credible, the lender will typically offer a waiver. Waivers can be unconditional or conditional — the lender may require additional reporting, a temporary covenant reset, or a fee. These are negotiable.

The founders who get the best outcomes are the ones who surface the issue before the compliance certificate is due. A proactive call changes the dynamic from "you failed a test" to "we are managing a situation."

When the conversation gets harder

The breach becomes a real problem when it coincides with other risk signals: declining revenue, management turnover, cash below a threshold, or a pattern of repeated misses. In those cases, the lender may tighten terms, require additional collateral, or begin a more formal remediation process.

Even then, acceleration is unusual. Lenders prefer amended terms to enforced collection. But the founder's leverage in that negotiation depends entirely on the quality of the financial information and the credibility of the plan.

What to prepare now

  • Model every covenant forward for the next four quarters. Know when a miss is possible before it happens.
  • Build a relationship with the lender before you need it. Regular reporting and proactive communication create trust that matters when something goes wrong.
  • If a breach looks likely within 60-90 days, call the lender. Do not wait for the compliance certificate to surface the issue.
  • Prepare a remediation package: cause, revised forecast, plan, and timeline. The quality of this package determines the quality of the waiver conversation.

Frequently asked questions

What happens when a SaaS company breaches a debt covenant?

The lender is notified or discovers the breach. In most cases, they ask what happened, what the plan is, and when the company expects to return to compliance. Acceleration is possible but uncommon when the borrower communicates proactively.

Can you negotiate a covenant waiver?

Yes. Lenders routinely grant waivers when the borrower surfaces the breach early, explains the cause, and presents a credible remediation plan. The conversation is usually about the plan, not the breach itself.

How should a founder prepare for a potential covenant breach?

Model the covenant metrics forward. If a breach looks likely in the next 60-90 days, notify the lender before it happens. Bring a plan that addresses the cause, a revised forecast showing the path back to compliance, and a timeline.
