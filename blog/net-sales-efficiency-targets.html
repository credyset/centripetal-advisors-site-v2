Set Revenue Targets From Spend, Not From Aspiration

Start with what you can actually spend on sales and marketing. Apply a net sales efficiency ratio. Work backwards to the CARR you can realistically buy. Then test whether the aspiration survives the math.

Direct answer: Revenue targets should be derived from sales and marketing spend multiplied by a net sales efficiency ratio, then decomposed into leads, conversion rates, and headcount. If the bottom-up math does not reach the top-down aspiration, either the aspiration or the spend needs to change.

The 3X net sales efficiency framework

Most revenue targets start with aspiration: the board wants a number, leadership commits to a number, the plan works backwards from a number. The problem is that the number often arrives before anyone tests whether the company can buy it.

A more useful starting point is the sales and marketing budget. Multiply the planned spend by a net sales efficiency ratio — typically 3X for a well-functioning SaaS GTM motion — and the result is the CARR the company can realistically expect to generate. That is the ceiling, not the floor.

A revenue target is not a plan until it is derived from what the company can actually spend and what that spend produces.Centripetal operating principle

Decompose the target into testable assumptions

Once the spend-derived target exists, break it down. Start with average ticket size and work outward: how many new customers does the target require? At the company's current conversion rate, how many qualified leads does that demand? How much pipeline needs to exist to produce those leads? How many salespeople, SDRs, or marketing programs does the pipeline require?

Each step is a test. If the company needs 200 new customers but has never closed more than 80 in a year, the plan has a capacity problem. If the required lead volume exceeds what the marketing budget can produce, the plan has a spend problem. If the average ticket size needs to double to make the numbers work, the plan has a pricing problem.

What the exercise reveals

The decomposition does more than validate a target. It reveals whether the company's GTM motion is operating at, above, or below efficiency — and that changes the fundraise narrative.

A company spending efficiently at 3X or above can tell investors it knows how to convert capital into revenue. A company operating below efficiency needs to explain what it is fixing before it can absorb more spend. Both are honest positions. The dangerous position is not knowing which one applies.

Replace "we need $X" with a testable acquisition model

  • Start with the planned sales and marketing budget for the period.
  • Apply the company's historical or target net sales efficiency ratio.
  • Derive the CARR the budget can realistically produce.
  • Decompose into customer count, ticket size, conversion rate, pipeline, and headcount.
  • Compare the bottom-up result to the top-down aspiration.
  • If they do not match, decide whether to change the target, increase the spend, or improve the efficiency.

The exercise replaces "we need $X ARR by year-end" with a model that can be tested, adjusted, and defended. It also makes the conversation with the board more precise: instead of debating whether the target is ambitious enough, the team can show what the spend is expected to produce and where the gaps are.

Frequently asked questions

What is net sales efficiency?

Net new ARR divided by the sales and marketing spend that produced it. A 3X ratio means every dollar spent generates three dollars of new annual recurring revenue.

How should a SaaS company set its annual revenue target?

Start with the planned sales and marketing budget, multiply by a realistic efficiency ratio, decompose into customer count, ticket size, and conversion rate. Test whether the result matches the aspiration before committing headcount.

What happens when the math does not reach the target?

Either the target needs to change, the spend needs to increase, or the efficiency ratio needs to improve. The exercise is designed to surface that gap before the year begins.
