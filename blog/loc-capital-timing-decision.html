The Line of Credit as a Capital Timing Tool

Most founders frame the capital decision as raise or don't raise. The third option — bridge with a line of credit to improve terms on the next round — is often the most underused.

A line of credit is not a substitute for equity and not a sign of weakness. It is a timing instrument: it lets a founder improve operating metrics, wait for better terms, and raise from a position of optionality rather than urgency. The decision to use one depends on whether the company has predictable revenue and a credible path to improved unit economics.

The three-way capital decision

Founders typically think about capital in two frames: raise now, or wait. But there is a third option that changes the negotiating dynamics entirely.

A line of credit allows the company to extend its runway without immediate dilution. The cost is interest and the obligation to repay — but the benefit is time: time to improve EBITDA, time to demonstrate traction, time to raise at a higher valuation when the metrics support it.

The question is not whether you need capital. The question is whether you need it today, at today's terms, or whether you can earn better terms by demonstrating 90 more days of operating progress.Centripetal operating principle

When the LOC bridge works

The LOC is a useful bridge when three conditions are true. First, the company has predictable contracted revenue — not aspirational pipeline. Second, there is a specific operating lever that will improve within the bridge period: gross margin, net retention, CAC payback, or burn multiple. Third, the founder has a clear thesis about why the next round will happen at better terms.

Without all three, the LOC is just deferred dilution with an additional repayment obligation.

When it does not

If the company is burning cash without a path to improved efficiency, borrowing extends the runway without changing the trajectory. The next equity round still happens — but now the company carries debt into the negotiation. That is a worse position, not a better one.

The LOC also does not replace the need for financial infrastructure. A lender will require financial reporting, covenant compliance, and cash visibility. If the company does not have those systems, the LOC application process itself will expose the gaps.

The leverage effect

Access to non-dilutive capital changes the fundraising conversation. A founder who can say "we have runway and we are choosing to raise" negotiates differently than a founder who needs to close before cash runs out. The LOC does not guarantee better terms, but it creates the conditions for a better negotiation.

What to prepare before the conversation

  • A cash and runway model that shows the bridge period and the expected improvement in operating metrics.
  • Monthly financials that a lender can review without rebuilding the close process.
  • A clear thesis about what changes in the next 90-180 days and why that justifies waiting.
  • An understanding of the LOC terms: interest, covenants, reporting requirements, and repayment schedule.

Frequently asked questions

When should a SaaS founder consider a line of credit instead of raising equity?

When the company has predictable revenue, a path to improved operating metrics, and a near-term reason to believe the next equity round would happen at better terms. The LOC bridges timing, not insolvency.

What is the risk of using a line of credit as a bridge?

The LOC must be repaid. If operating metrics do not improve as expected, the company may face the same equity raise it was trying to defer, with the additional obligation of servicing or repaying the LOC.

How does a line of credit change fundraising leverage?

Having access to non-dilutive capital gives the founder optionality: the ability to wait for better terms, negotiate from a position of runway rather than urgency, and demonstrate capital discipline to future investors.
