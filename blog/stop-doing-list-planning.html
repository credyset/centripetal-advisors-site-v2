Every Planning Session Should Produce a Stop-Doing List

What you cut is often more strategic than what you add. The stop-doing list is the counterweight to the priority list — and most planning sessions skip it entirely.

Direct answer: A planning session that produces only priorities is incomplete. The stop-doing list forces the team to name what it will stop doing to create capacity for new work. Without it, the new initiative competes with every existing commitment, and the team gets busier without getting more focused.

The pattern most planning sessions follow

A board meeting, annual plan, or quarterly review typically ends with a list of priorities, owners, dates, and metrics. The team leaves with more work to do. What the session rarely produces is a list of what the team will stop doing.

That omission is not accidental. Stopping work feels like failure. It implies the original decision was wrong, or the investment did not pay off, or someone's project was not important enough to continue. So teams avoid the conversation, and the list of active commitments only grows.

Why the stop-doing list matters

Every new priority needs capacity. If the team does not name what it will trade away, the new initiative competes with everything else for the same people, the same hours, and the same attention. The result is not more output — it is more motion with less progress.

The stop-doing list is the capacity test. It asks: what is the team willing to give up to make this new thing work? If the answer is nothing, then the team is either undercommitted (unlikely) or unwilling to make the trade-off (common).

What the stop-doing list surfaces

The exercise reveals organizational fear. Teams resist cutting things they started because stopping feels like a judgment on the people who launched them. But continuing work that no longer earns its place is more expensive than acknowledging the trade-off. The cost is not just the direct spend — it is the management attention, the coordination overhead, and the opportunity cost of keeping something alive that is not producing results.

The stop-doing list also reveals misalignment. When two leaders disagree on what to cut, the disagreement usually reflects a deeper difference in strategy that the planning session has not resolved. That is useful information. It is better to surface it during planning than to discover it mid-quarter when both initiatives are underfunded.

How to build the list

Test each active initiative against three criteria:

  • Ownership: Is there a named person accountable for the outcome? If not, the initiative is running on inertia.
  • Contribution: Can the team measure what this work produces? If the contribution is assumed but not tracked, the initiative may not be earning its place.
  • Capacity: Does the team have the people, time, and budget to execute this alongside the new priorities? If not, something has to move.

Initiatives that fail all three tests belong on the stop-doing list. Initiatives that fail one or two deserve a conversation about whether they should be paused, scoped down, or ended.

What to leave every planning session with

  • Named owners for every priority.
  • Dates and milestones, not just objectives.
  • Metrics that define progress, not activity.
  • A meeting cadence for review, not just a kickoff.
  • A stop-doing list with the same specificity as the priority list.

If the planning session cannot produce a stop-doing list, the team has not yet made the decisions the plan requires. The priorities are aspirations until the trade-offs are named.

Frequently asked questions

What is a stop-doing list?

A list of activities, initiatives, or commitments the team will deliberately end or pause to create capacity for new work. It is the complement to a priority list.

Why do planning sessions skip the stop-doing list?

Because stopping work feels like admitting a past decision was wrong. But continuing work that no longer earns its place is more expensive than acknowledging the trade-off.

How do you decide what to stop?

Test each initiative against capacity, ownership, and contribution. If there is no named owner, no measurable contribution, or no capacity to execute, it belongs on the stop-doing list.
