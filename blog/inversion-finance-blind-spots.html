The Finance Blind Spot Question: What Did We Not Know?

Instead of asking "what is working," ask "what information did we not have that cost us?" The Munger inversion applied to startup finance — and the answer is usually not exotic.

Direct answer: The most useful annual finance review question is not what went well, but what information the company lacked that would have changed a decision. The answer is usually visibility into CARR by customer, cash-basis versus accrual confusion, or headcount costs with raise dates — not exotic gaps.

Inversion as a finance tool

Charlie Munger's inversion principle — "invert, always invert" — is often applied to investment decisions. But it is equally useful in operating finance. Instead of asking what the finance function accomplished this year, ask what it failed to surface.

The value of inversion in finance is that it focuses attention on the information system, not the outcome. A company that lost a large customer is not necessarily running badly — but a company that lost a large customer and did not know the concentration risk existed has a visibility problem.

The usual blind spots

The gaps that inversion reveals are rarely exotic. They tend to fall into a few categories:

CARR visibility by customer

Many early-stage SaaS companies track total ARR or MRR without breaking it down by customer. This makes it difficult to see concentration risk, identify customers likely to churn, or understand the revenue impact of losing a single account.

Cash-basis versus accrual confusion

When the team looks at cash in the bank and assumes it reflects profitability, or when accrual-basis financials mask a cash timing problem, decisions get made on the wrong version of reality. The confusion is common and the consequences are real.

Headcount costs without raise timing

A headcount plan that does not include raise dates, bonus timing, or benefits cost increases will understate the true cost of the team. The gap compounds over the year and often surfaces as a budget variance that was predictable but not predicted.

Customer concentration

The risk of having a large percentage of revenue from a small number of customers is well understood in theory. In practice, many companies do not track it until after a churn event forces the question.

From retrospective to diagnostic

The standard annual review asks: what went well, what did not, what will we do differently? These are useful questions. But they focus on outcomes, which are partially outside the company's control.

The inversion question focuses on the information system, which is within the company's control. If the company did not have CARR by customer, that is a reporting decision that can be fixed. If headcount costs did not include raise timing, that is a planning process that can be improved.

The answers to the inversion question become next year's reporting and data priorities. They are specific, actionable, and tied to decisions that were actually made — not hypothetical improvements.

How to run the exercise

  • After the standard annual review, add one question to each function: what information did we not have that would have changed a decision?
  • Collect the answers. Look for patterns across functions — often the same visibility gap affects multiple teams.
  • Prioritize the gaps by decision impact, not by difficulty. The gap that affected a hiring decision or a pricing decision matters more than the one that would have improved a report.
  • Build the fix into next year's reporting cadence, not as a one-time project.

Frequently asked questions

What is inversion thinking in finance?

Asking what you did not know, rather than what you accomplished. It surfaces visibility gaps in the finance system that caused or worsened problems.

What are common blind spots in SaaS finance?

CARR visibility by customer, cash-basis versus accrual confusion, headcount costs without factoring raise timing, and customer concentration that only surfaces after a churn event.

How do you apply inversion in an annual review?

After reviewing results, ask each function: what information did we not have that would have changed a decision? The answers become next year's reporting and data priorities.
